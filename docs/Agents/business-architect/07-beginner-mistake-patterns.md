# Beginner Mistake Patterns: What Goes Wrong and How to Catch It Early

## Overview
This document catalogs the 10 most damaging mistakes beginners make, organized by category. For each mistake: the behavior, the psychology that makes it feel rational, what happens, the fix, and when the mistake is actually right. The GPT uses this to proactively catch mistakes in conversation by listening for early-warning language patterns.

## How to Use This Document
The GPT should treat conversations as early-warning pattern matching. When it detects "certainty-seeking language" (listed under each mistake), it should immediately switch into evidence-seeking questions rather than letting the user proceed down a dead-end path.

## ICP Mistakes

### Mistake: "My ICP is everyone who might pay"
**The psychology:** "If I pick a tight niche and I'm wrong, I'll waste months. Staying broad keeps my options open."
**What happens:** Messaging becomes generic. Sales gets dragged into educating bad-fit prospects. High churn because expectations don't match reality.
**The fix:** Define ICP by best-fit criteria (who feels your value most) plus disqualifiers you'll walk away from. Let bad deals drop out through clear positioning.
**When it's actually right:** Early discovery (not selling) where you're mapping multiple segments — but only if time-boxed and labeled as exploration.
**Early-warning language:** "I don't want to limit myself," "anyone who needs help with…," "my service works for everyone."
**Source:** April Dunford.

### Mistake: Choosing niche based on passion/identity, assuming the market will reward authenticity
**The psychology:** "If I love it, I'll stay motivated. If it's my calling, customers will feel the energy."
**What happens:** Months building something with no funded demand. Momentum dies when interest doesn't convert.
**The fix:** Replace "passion-first" with "craft + pull": start from observable pains, build competence, let passion emerge from capability and market response.
**When it's actually right:** When you have genuine unfair advantage (rare expertise, privileged buyer access) AND still validate willingness-to-pay.
**Early-warning language:** "This is my passion," "I've always wanted to help people with…," "I feel called to…"
**Source:** Cal Newport.

## Offer Mistakes

### Mistake: Copying competitor packages instead of clarifying unique positioning
**The psychology:** "If I copy what already sells, I can't be wrong — and I won't have to defend my choices."
**What happens:** Prospects compare you to "lookalikes." Funnel friction rises. You drift into price competition.
**The fix:** Start with your "secret sauce": what you do better, who values that most, what problem-aware language they use. Design an offer that promises a crisp, testable outcome.
**When it's actually right:** Intentionally entering a commodity market as a low-cost operator with sustainable cost advantage.
**Early-warning language:** "I looked at what [competitor] charges," "the standard package is…," "everyone offers this."
**Source:** Michael Porter / April Dunford.

### Mistake: Over-complicating the first offer (custom everything, multiple business models)
**The psychology:** "If I customize, I'll close more deals. I don't have to say no."
**What happens:** Delivery becomes impossible to systematize. Sales cycles elongate. You become the bottleneck.
**The fix:** Name and standardize a "service that looks like a product" — consistent scope, method, and outcome. If you change models, make it explicit.
**When it's actually right:** High-end bespoke engagements where customization IS the product and margins fund white-glove delivery.
**Early-warning language:** "It depends on the client," "I'll customize based on what they need," "I can do a bit of everything."
**Source:** John Warrillow / Built to Sell.

## Pricing Mistakes

### Mistake: Pricing by the hour because it feels "fair"
**The psychology:** "If I charge for time, I can justify my price and avoid the fear that I'm not worth it."
**What happens:** You get treated like labor. Incentives misalign (clients police minutes; you're punished for efficiency). Income ceiling tied to calendar.
**The fix:** Sell expertise and outcomes. Define the business result, price the engagement (not hours), treat internal time as cost — not the thing you sell.
**When it's actually right:** True time-and-materials work with undefined scope (short discovery phase) where buyer explicitly wants time-based billing with guardrails.
**Early-warning language:** "I charge $X per hour," "how do I figure out my hourly rate," "clients want to know my rate."
**Source:** Jonathan Stark.

### Mistake: Underpricing or discounting to win first clients
**The psychology:** "I need a quick yes to prove I'm legit. Once I have a testimonial, I'll raise prices."
**What happens:** You train the account to be price-driven. Set a benchmark you rarely climb out of. Attract buyers who won't become loyal value-buyers.
**The fix:** Don't discount to win. If price is the obstacle, verify it's truly the obstacle first. If you must concede, trade scope or terms — not price. Reserve discounts for proven loyal clients.
**When it's actually right:** A clearly labeled, time-boxed beta (limited slots, explicit learning goals, written "price resets after beta").
**Early-warning language:** "I'll start low and raise later," "I just need one client to prove myself," "I don't have enough experience to charge that."
**Source:** Blair Enns / Win Without Pitching.

## Validation Mistakes

### Mistake: Over-researching and delaying launch (perfectionism disguised as rigor)
**The psychology:** "If I just think a bit more, I can avoid mistakes." Planning feels productive and emotionally safer than exposing a rough version to real users.
**What happens:** You lose the highest-value feedback window. Assumptions calcify. Timelines slip.
**The fix:** Ship a "quantum of utility" and use direct user contact as the product engine. Choose speed unless the domain has serious penalties for mistakes.
**When it's actually right:** Regulated, safety-critical, or trust-critical contexts where a mistake has outsized harm.
**Early-warning language:** "I'm almost ready," "I just need to finish my website first," "I want to make sure it's perfect before I launch."
**Source:** Paul Graham.

### Mistake: "Validating" by asking friends and family instead of real prospects
**The psychology:** "My friends will be honest — and it's faster than cold outreach." Approval feels like evidence.
**What happens:** False positives ("sounds amazing!"). You misread compliments as demand and build the wrong thing.
**The fix:** Follow Mom Test rules: talk about their life, ask for concrete past behavior, push toward commitments (preorder, deposit, scheduled next step) rather than opinions.
**When it's actually right:** When "friends" are legitimately in your target market AND you still avoid pitching and ask for past behavior/commitments.
**Early-warning language:** "My friends think it's a great idea," "everyone I've talked to loves it," "people keep telling me I should do this."
**Source:** Rob Fitzpatrick / The Mom Test.

## Acquisition Mistakes

### Mistake: Building brand (website, social profiles, content) before getting even one client
**The psychology:** "If I look professional, clients will come. Marketing is broadcasting."
**What happens:** You wait for inbound that hasn't been earned yet. Learn nothing about real buyer language. Delay cashflow.
**The fix:** Start with direct contact and learning. Identify people who recently bought what you sell, ask why they hired, what triggered the purchase. Turn patterns into a narrow offer.
**When it's actually right:** When the product IS attention (creator/media businesses) — but even then, you need tight positioning.
**Early-warning language:** "I need a website first," "I'm working on my branding," "I'll start reaching out once my Instagram is ready."
**Source:** Paul Jarvis.

### Mistake: Spending on ads before having a proven offer
**The psychology:** "Growth will fix it — once people see it, they'll buy." Paid feels like control.
**What happens:** You amplify a leaky bucket. Low engagement + unclear positioning = fast cash burn.
**The fix:** Treat paid as a multiplier, not a finder. First prove the offer with organic/direct acquisition. Track unit economics. Only scale spend once you can predictably convert and retain.
**When it's actually right:** Small-budget ads as a deliberate validation tool (smoke test) where the goal is learning and you can tolerate losing the budget.
**Early-warning language:** "I'll run some Facebook ads," "I just need more traffic," "the problem is visibility."
**Source:** Unusual Ventures.

## GPT Behavior Rules Based on This Document

1. **When detecting early-warning language:** Don't lecture. Ask a pointed question that surfaces the real situation. Example: User says "I don't want to limit my audience" → GPT asks "If your business depended on getting 3 clients this week, who specifically would you target?"

2. **When the user is making a mistake that's actually right for their situation:** Acknowledge the edge case. "That approach CAN work if [condition]. Is that your situation?"

3. **Never be a yes-man.** If the user's idea has a clear problem (no paying ICP, undifferentiated offer, passion-only niche), say so directly but constructively. Frame it as "let's pressure-test this" not "you're wrong."

4. **Prioritize the earliest mistake.** If ICP is wrong, don't help with pricing. Fix the foundation first.
