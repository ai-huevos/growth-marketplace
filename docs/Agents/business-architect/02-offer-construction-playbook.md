# Offer Construction Playbook: Turning ICP + Skill Into a Sellable Package

## Overview
This document covers how to construct a concrete, sellable offer once the ICP is defined. An offer is not "what you do" — it's a packaged promise of a specific outcome, delivered through a specific mechanism, in a specific timeframe, with specific risk reversal. The GPT uses these frameworks to walk beginners from "I help people with X" to "Here's exactly what I'm selling, to whom, for how much, delivered how."

## Core Principle: Offers Are Bridges, Not Menus
A weak offer lists activities ("6 coaching calls, email support, worksheets"). A strong offer promises a transformation and packages the activities as the bridge to get there. Beginners default to menus because they feel safer. The GPT must redirect to transformation-first packaging.

## The Value Equation (Hormozi)
Every offer's perceived value is determined by four levers. The GPT should optimize all four:

**Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort/Sacrifice)**

- **Dream Outcome**: What's the best possible result? Make it vivid and specific.
- **Perceived Likelihood**: How confident is the buyer it will work for THEM? (Testimonials, guarantees, process transparency, milestones)
- **Time Delay**: How fast do they see results? (Faster = more valuable)
- **Effort/Sacrifice**: How much work does the buyer have to do? (Less = more valuable)

**GPT Question Sequence:**
- "What transformation does your client want? Describe their 'after' state."
- "How can we make this feel more certain to work? (Process proof, milestones, guarantees)"
- "How can we make this faster?"
- "How can we make this easier / require less from the client?"

## The Obstacle-to-Solution Method (Hormozi Offer Checklist)
The most reliable way to build offer components:

**Step 1 — List every obstacle between the client and the outcome:**
- "What stops people from achieving [the outcome] on their own?"
- "What have they already tried that didn't work? Why did it fail?"
- "What are they afraid of?"
- "What habits or routines get in the way?"

**Step 2 — Turn each obstacle into an included solution:**
For each obstacle, ask: "How can we make this easier / faster / more enjoyable / more likely to work?"

**Step 3 — Choose delivery format using the Delivery Cube:**

| Dimension | Options |
|-----------|---------|
| Done level | DIY (they do it) / DWY (done-with-you) / DFY (done-for-you) |
| Format | 1:1 / Small group / Course / Community / Templates |
| Support | Email / Chat / Calls / Async video |
| Response speed | Same-day / 24hr / Weekly |

**Step 4 — Trim and Stack:**
- Keep mostly "high value, low cost to deliver" components (templates, frameworks, group calls)
- Add 1-2 "high value, high cost" components that make it feel premium (1:1 element, custom deliverable)
- Cut anything that doesn't directly serve the transformation

## Minimum Viable Offer (MVO) Framework (Rmosh)
For the FIRST version of the offer — keep it small, testable, and sellable:

**Four required elements:**
1. **One clear promise** (not three promises — ONE)
2. **Concrete outcomes** (measurable or observable)
3. **A timeframe** (when do they see results?)
4. **A price point** (even if it's a "beta" price)

**MVO Template:**
"In [timeframe], I help [ICP] achieve [specific outcome] through [mechanism/process]. It includes [2-3 key deliverables]. Investment: $[price]."

**Example:** "In 8 weeks, I help freelance designers land 3 new clients through a proven outreach system. It includes weekly strategy calls, custom outreach templates, and a done-with-you prospecting sprint. Investment: $1,500."

**Critical rule:** Test the MVO with real outreach BEFORE building anything elaborate. Validation is behavior (payment, booked calls) not opinions ("sounds great!").

## The Micro Offer (Dan Koe) — For Absolute Beginners
When the user can't commit to a full program yet:

**Step 1 — Identify the smallest unit of value:**
- "What's the smallest thing you could do for someone that would genuinely help them?"
- "Could you deliver that in a single session, a week, or 30 days?"

**Step 2 — Package with a unique process name:**
- Give your method a name (e.g., "The 3-Part Launch System," "The Revenue Reset Sprint")
- A named process feels more valuable than "I'll help you with marketing"

**Step 3 — Use a qualifying questionnaire as sales filter:**
- "What are your biggest struggles with [topic]?"
- "Where do you want to be 30 days from now?"
- "Have you invested money in solving this before?"
- "How serious are you about fixing this (1-10)?"

## Offer Construction from Switch Forces (Thomson/JTBD)
When the ICP was defined using the switch-moment method:

**Build the offer so that:**
- The **promise** matches what pulled them (the "Pull" force)
- The **packaging** removes what scared them (the "Anxiety" force)
- The **delivery** replaces what they'd lose (the "Habit" force)
- The **positioning** names what pushed them away from the old solution (the "Push" force)

**Example:** ICP switched from a fitness app (Push: no accountability, quit after 2 weeks). Offer: "4-week accountability sprint" with daily check-ins (replaces Habit of app-based tracking), clear week-2 milestone (reduces Anxiety about wasting money), and "extend support free if you don't hit adherence target" (removes risk).

## Differentiation-First Offer Design (Dunford)
When the user has a unique method or background:

**Step 1:** "What do you do differently from how most people solve this?"
**Step 2:** "What outcome does your different approach make possible?"
**Step 3:** Package the offer around the DIFFERENCE, not the category.

**Anti-pattern:** "I'm a marketing consultant" (generic category, invites comparison).
**Differentiated:** "I help B2B founders get their first 10 clients through direct outreach — no content, no ads, no waiting" (specific mechanism, specific outcome, implicit comparison to slower methods).

## Offer Naming and Positioning
A good offer name:
- Names the outcome or transformation (not the process)
- Includes a timeframe or speed signal
- Sounds like a PRODUCT, not a person's time

**Weak names:** "Business Coaching Package," "Marketing Help," "Strategy Sessions"
**Strong names:** "The 30-Day Client Engine," "Revenue Reset Sprint," "First 10 Clients System"

## When to Challenge the User's Offer
Push back when:
- The offer is a list of activities with no promised outcome → Ask "what transformation does this produce?"
- The offer tries to solve everything → Force "what's the ONE result?"
- The offer has no timeframe → Ask "when does the client see results?"
- The offer has no price → Help them price it (see pricing doc)
- The offer copies competitors exactly → Use Dunford's differentiation sequence
- The offer is over-customized → Push toward standardized packaging

## Key Signals That Offer Construction Is Complete
- There is ONE clear promise (not multiple)
- The outcome is specific and measurable (or at least observable)
- There is a defined timeframe
- The delivery format is chosen (DIY/DWY/DFY)
- There is a price (even if beta)
- The offer has a name
- It can be explained in 2 sentences
