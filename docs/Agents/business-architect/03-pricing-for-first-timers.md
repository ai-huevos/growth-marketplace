# Pricing for First-Timers: How to Set Your First Price

## Overview
This document contains five proven step-by-step pricing processes for beginners with zero sales history. Each process includes the specific questions to ask, calculations to run, and how to handle the psychological barriers (imposter syndrome, fear of charging "too much"). The GPT uses these to walk users through their first pricing decision conversationally.

## Core Principle: Your First Price Is a Hypothesis, Not a Commitment
Beginners freeze because they think pricing must be "right." It doesn't. The first price is a testable hypothesis. If it's wrong, you adjust. The goal is to set a defensible starting point fast, then iterate based on real buyer response.

## Process 1: Floor Price From Salary Benchmark (Zipursky)
Best for: Service providers and consultants who can anchor to a comparable salary.

**Questions the GPT asks:**
1. "What job role is closest to what you'll be doing for clients?"
2. "What's the average salary for that role? (If you don't know, I can help estimate)"
3. "How many weeks per year will you work? (Use 48 for planning)"
4. "How many BILLABLE hours per week realistically? (Use 20 for beginners — the rest is admin, marketing, learning)"

**Calculation:**
- Hourly baseline = Annual salary ÷ (working weeks × billable hours/week)
- Project fee = (Estimated hours for deliverables) × hourly rate × 1.5 buffer
- The 1.5x buffer accounts for revisions, admin, and "projects take longer than you think"

**Example:** $98,000 salary benchmark → ~$100/hr. 30 hours of deliverables → $3,000. Apply 1.5x → **$4,500 starting project price.**

**When the user says "that feels too high":** "Round to a number you can say out loud without flinching. If you feel underpaid, raise it. The guardrail is: don't lower your fee to win a deal."

Source: Michael Zipursky / Consulting Success.

## Process 2: Calendar-Capacity Pricing (Shannon Mattern)
Best for: Designers, freelancers, coaches — anyone with limited time slots.

**Questions the GPT asks:**
1. "How much do you need to pay yourself per month (after taxes)?"
2. "What are your monthly business costs (tools, subscriptions, training)?"
3. "How many client projects or spots can you handle per month? Be realistic — block personal time, marketing, and admin first."

**Calculation:**
- Monthly revenue needed = (Monthly pay goal + business costs) ÷ (1 - tax rate estimate)
- Use 22% as a starting tax estimate if unsure
- Minimum price per project = Monthly revenue needed ÷ available monthly spots

**Example:** $5,000/month pay goal + $1,500 business costs = $6,500. At 22% tax rate → ~$8,750/month needed. If you can handle 3 projects/month → **minimum $2,917 per project.**

**When the user says "that's more than I'm charging now":** "Would you take out your wallet and personally pay the difference for this client? If not, you're subsidizing their project with your own money."

**Key insight:** This method avoids imposter syndrome by treating price as capacity math, not self-worth assessment.

Source: Shannon Mattern / Web Designer Academy.

## Process 3: Value-Based Three-Option Proposal (Jonathan Stark)
Best for: Consultants and service providers whose work has measurable business impact.

**Questions the GPT asks:**
1. "What's the business result this client would get? (Revenue increase, time saved, cost reduced, risk avoided)"
2. "What's that worth to them annually? Even a rough estimate."
3. "Why do they need this NOW? What changed?"
4. "Why would they hire YOU vs. doing it themselves or hiring someone cheaper?"

**Calculation (price-first, scope-second):**
- Estimate the value of the outcome to the client
- Set three price tiers BEFORE defining scope:
  - Option 1 (entry): ~10% of estimated value
  - Option 2 (recommended): ~20% of estimated value
  - Option 3 (premium): ~50% of estimated value
- Then design scope to fit each tier

**Example:** Client outcome worth ~$100K/year → Three options: $10K / $22K / $50K. Define what you'd deliver at each level.

**For smaller-scale beginners:** Same logic, smaller numbers. Client outcome worth ~$5K → Options: $500 / $1,000 / $2,500.

**When the user says "I can't charge that much":** "We're not pricing YOUR time. We're pricing THEIR outcome. If the result is worth $5K to them, charging $500 is fair and they're getting 10x value."

**Key insight:** You don't build confidence through pep talks. You build it by forcing the prospect (and yourself) to articulate value BEFORE discussing deliverables.

Source: Jonathan Stark.

## Process 4: Beta Pricing Ladder (Stephanie Fiteni)
Best for: Coaches, course creators, anyone who needs to start with zero testimonials.

**Questions the GPT asks:**
1. "What are comparable providers charging? (Don't copy them — use as market anchor)"
2. "What's your desired annual income?"
3. "How many clients can you serve per month?"

**Calculation:**
- Monthly income goal = Annual target ÷ 12
- Minimum package price = Monthly income goal ÷ monthly client capacity
- Add margin for prep, admin, materials

**The Beta Framework:**
- Set a "Beta Price" you can say out loud without wobbling
- Frame it explicitly: "Beta pricing — I'm taking [X] clients at this rate while I refine the program"
- Commit to raising after a set number of clients (e.g., after 10)
- NEVER frame discount as permanent or deserved — frame as strategic and time-limited

**Example trajectory:** Start at $650 for 6 sessions → raise to $1,200 after 5 clients → raise to $2,500 after 10 clients → reach $3,997 for a 3-month package.

**When the user says "I have no proof, I can't charge real money":** "'Beta' solves this. It means 'valuable but evolving.' Don't raise to a level that freaks you out and you can't sell — price must be sellable by your nervous system, not just the spreadsheet."

Source: Stephanie Fiteni.

## Process 5: Effective Hourly Rate Check (Lindsay Marsh)
Best for: Freelancers who aren't sure whether to charge hourly or fixed.

**Decision rule for hourly vs. fixed:**
- "Is this the first time you're doing this type of deliverable?" → Start hourly (you don't know how long it takes yet)
- "Do you know exactly how long this takes you?" → Switch to fixed (capture efficiency gains)

**Questions the GPT asks:**
1. "How many hours do you estimate for this project?"
2. "What's a reasonable hourly rate for your experience level in this market?"

**Calculation:**
- Fixed quote = estimated hours × hourly rate
- ALWAYS check: Effective hourly rate = actual fee ÷ actual hours spent
- If effective hourly drops below your target, either raise price or tighten scope

**Example:** Beginner charges $20/post × 10 posts = $200. Spends 20 hours → effective rate is $10/hr. At $40/hr target, should charge $400 minimum.

**Context variables to factor in:** Project value to client, your experience level, client size, project exposure/portfolio value, timeline urgency, and current demand for your services.

Source: Lindsay Marsh.

## Pricing Psychology: What the GPT Should Know

**Common emotional patterns to watch for:**
- "I'll charge less now and raise later" → Rarely works. Low price attracts price-sensitive buyers who won't accept increases.
- "I need to be fair" → "Fair" usually means "discounted." Value-based pricing IS fair — the client gets more value than they pay.
- "What if they say no?" → Price objections often mean the offer isn't clear, not that the price is wrong.
- "I'm not experienced enough" → Experience affects speed, not value. A beginner who delivers the outcome is worth the outcome price.

**The GPT should never say:** "Charge what you're worth." This triggers more imposter syndrome. Instead: "Let's calculate what the outcome is worth to the client, then price based on that."

## When Pricing Conversation Is Complete
- User has a specific number (not a range)
- The number is defensible (tied to value, capacity, or market benchmark — not feelings)
- User can explain the price to a prospect without apologizing
- There's a plan for when/how to raise (beta ladder, milestone triggers, or quarterly review)
