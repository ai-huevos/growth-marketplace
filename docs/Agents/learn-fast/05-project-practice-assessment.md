# Project-Based Learning, Practice Design, and Self-Assessment

## Overview
Frameworks for designing the right projects at the right time, advanced practice techniques that accelerate beyond standard repetition, and self-assessment methods for learners without a teacher. Projects are primary learning vehicles, not afterthoughts.

## How does 4C/ID whole-task scaffolding sequence projects?
Build the roadmap out of "whole tasks" (authentic projects) grouped into task classes moving from simple-to-complex. Within each class, support starts high and fades (sawtooth pattern), while variability stays high to promote transfer. Add just-in-time procedural info during performance and reserve high-repetition drills for routine subskills only after they appear inside the whole task. Best for complex, real-world skills where coordination matters. Advance when you can complete "empty" tasks (no scaffolds) at the current complexity level.

## How should deliberate practice shape project design?
Treat each project as a test of a narrow, well-defined performance goal, not a broad "build X." Identify the current constraint (the thing that fails first), design a micro-project forcing repeated attempts at that constraint, and require immediate actionable feedback. Keep iterations short enough that adjustments happen while failure is fresh. Use "error logs" plus a single metric per slice. Reassess the same slice later for retention, not just same-day gains.

## How does copy-then-create progression work?
Start by reconstructing high-quality existing work (copy with understanding), shift to completion problems (fill missing steps), then to full original creation. The "fade" is the design: learners gradually supply more of the solution. Domains: programming, algebra, music sight-reading, drawing fundamentals. Compare against the original and score differences. When copying no longer produces new error discoveries, the task is under-challenging and should be faded forward.

## How should capstone and portfolio projects be designed?
Define the "transfer performance" first: what the learner should do in a new, messy situation. Design a capstone forcing integration across modules requiring evidence plus reflection. Align earlier projects to capstone criteria so the roadmap is not disconnected builds. Use integrative rubrics scoring transfer and reflection, not just the final product. The portfolio is the self-directed learner's substitute for a curriculum committee: it makes gaps visible.

## How does productive failure improve conceptual learning?
Put learners into a hard problem before formal instruction, constrained so they generate multiple solution attempts and surface misconceptions. Then follow with explicit consolidation. The benefit is not the failure. It is the preparation for learning and the contrast between flawed and canonical models. Best for concept-rich domains: statistics, physics, design tradeoffs. Require learners to submit attempted models, predicted failure points, and post-instruction error analysis.

## How do contrast cases and interleaving train discrimination and transfer?
Use paired comparisons differing on one deep feature at a time, then mix problem types so the learner must choose the right method. Add controlled variability to create contextual interference: practice feels worse, retention and transfer improve. Score two things separately: execution accuracy and method-selection accuracy. Interleaving benefits show up most in delayed tests and novel cases, not same-session fluency.

## How does teaching-as-learning (protégé effect) work?
Make the project output a teachable explanation. Preparing to teach changes how people encode and organize knowledge, even before any teaching happens. Use "Feynman-style" constraints: plain language, examples, explicit identification of gaps. Especially good when learners plateau because they can "do" but cannot explain why. Add adversarial questions (edge cases, counterexamples) and track which questions break the explanation.

## How should retrieval practice with calibration be used for self-assessment?
Replace "review" with recall: frequent low-stakes tests, ideally free recall or generation before checking answers. Add calibration: record confidence after each answer, compare to correctness. Track two dashboards: delayed recall score by concept, and calibration error (confidence minus accuracy). Improvement means both higher recall and tighter calibration.

## How do rubrics plus exemplars enable self-grading?
Use a rubric describing levels of performance, anchored with exemplars (what "good" looks like at each level). Add peer assessment to reduce solo blind spots and force articulation of criteria. Do agreement checks: do your rubric scores converge with peers over time?

## How can AI be used as an assessment partner?
Treat the model as a second rater, not an oracle. Provide the rubric, a few human-scored examples, and the learner artifact. Request a score plus citations to rubric criteria and a list of specific revisions. Triangulate: compare AI score to self-score, at least one peer/community score, and a delayed re-grade after revision. Use multiple prompts or judges to sanity-check; biases exist.

## Right project at the right stage
EARLY STAGE: Default to copy-then-create and heavy scaffolding. Limiting factor is cognitive overload, not motivation. Worked examples, completion tasks, fast feedback.
MIDDLE STAGE: Shift to whole-task sequences. Keep projects authentic but simplify conditions. Fade supports. Add deliberate-practice slices when progress stalls.
LATE STAGE: Capstones as transfer tests, not showcases. Integration across contexts, explicit reflection scored by integrative rubric. Portfolio makes gaps visible.
ALL STAGES: Feedback loop must include objective checks (retrieval tests, rubrics with exemplars) because self-assessment alone is systematically unreliable.
