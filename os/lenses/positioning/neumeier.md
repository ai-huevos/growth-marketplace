# Marty Neumeier — 3 - Strategic Positioning

> In any saturated marketplace, the only sustainable strategy is radical differentiation. A brand must be the ONLY one that does what it does, or it has no defensible position. Traditional differentiation yields pint-sized profits; radical differentiation creates ownable space that delivers profits over years.

*Active period*: 1990s–present

## Operating beliefs
- A brand is a person's gut feeling, companies influence it, can't dictate it.
- If you can't use the word ONLY, you don't have a zag.
- People seek tribal identity, not features: 'If I buy this, what does that make me?'
- Best practices are common practices. Common practices never add up to a zag.
- Naming is strategy, not creative.
- Trust = Reliability + Delight.
- Customer loyalty cannot be programmed.

## Key questions (use as self-check before finalizing output)
- What category does this brand claim?
- Can they complete: 'Our [offering] is the ONLY [category] that [benefit]'?
- Who shares the brandscape?
- Who is the target tribe and what identity do they adopt?
- Who/what is the enemy?
- Core purpose in 12 words or less?
- What trend are they riding?

## Biases (disclose in output footer)
- Design-world origin: overweights aesthetic expression vs. operational/financial dimensions.
- Differentiation absolutism: cost leadership and platform/ecosystem strategies structurally undervalued.
- Assumes resources for full touchpoint redesign.
- Swap/Hand tests favor visually distinctive brands, which isn't equally important across categories.
- Favors bold radical moves over measured incremental improvement.

## Limitations (suppress lens application in these contexts)
- Structurally underrates brands succeeding via trust, reliability, or price leadership (Kirkland, Walmart).
- Structurally underrates platform/ecosystem brands with network effects (AWS, Windows, Android).
- Does not validate whether differentiation is valued by the market, brand can be 'the only' doing something nobody wants.
- Does not assess economic viability of the claimed position.
- Unfairly penalizes me-too positions in genuinely underserved markets.
- Unfairly penalizes B2B service firms where proof of competence > unique claim.

## Frameworks

### The Onlyness Test
**Source**: Zag (2006) Step 6; martyneumeier.com

**Purpose**: Does the brand have a genuine, articulable point of radical differentiation?

Template-based test for radical differentiation with substitution verification.

#### Components
- **Basic Template** — Our [offering] is the ONLY [category] that [benefit/differentiator].
  - Key questions:
    - Can the brand complete this template cleanly?
    - Is the differentiator substantive and valued by the target?
- **Extended Template** — Our [offering] is the ONLY [WHAT: category] that [HOW: differentiator] for [WHO: target] in [WHERE: market] who [WHY: need] during [WHEN: trend].
  - Key questions:
    - Can all six elements be filled in coherently?
    - Does each element reinforce the others?
- **Substitution Test** — Replace brand name with competitor's. PASS = statement becomes false/absurd. FAIL = still works.
  - Key questions:
    - Does the statement become false/absurd with primary competitor's name?
    - Does it hold against secondary competitors?
    - Is the uniqueness trivial (technically only, but irrelevant to buying decisions)?
    - Can it be copied overnight by a competitor changing a surface attribute?
    - Is the white space actually abandoned territory nobody wants (false zag)?

#### Decision rules
- IF cannot complete basic template → fundamental flaw
- IF passes substitution + differentiator addresses real need → PASS
- IF passes substitution but differentiator is trivial → WEAK PASS
- IF fails substitution with primary competitor → critical weakness if combined with other failures

### The Trueline + Comma/And Diagnostic
**Source**: Zag (2006) Step 11; Level C Masterclass

**Purpose**: Can the value proposition be expressed as a single focused proposition competitors can't claim?

The trueline is the 'one true thing you can say about your brand', internal, strategic, derived from Onlyness. NOT customer-facing (that's the tagline). Converts to tagline for external use.

#### Components
- **Trueline Extraction** — Internal single-proposition brand truth. No commas, no 'ands.' Strategic tool, not customer-facing.
  - Key questions:
    - Can a single-proposition trueline be extracted?
    - How many commas and 'ands' does it contain? (0 = focused, 1 = partial, 3+ = fatal)
    - Could any competitor say it? (If yes, it's a category descriptor, not a trueline)
- **Trueline-to-Tagline Conversion** — Trueline (internal/strategic) converts to tagline (external/creative).
  - Key questions:
    - Does the trueline convert naturally to a customer-facing tagline?
    - Example: Southwest trueline 'fly for less than driving' → tagline 'You are now free to move about the country.'

#### Decision rules
- IF 0 commas, 0 ands → PASS, single proposition
- IF 1 comma or and → PARTIAL, two related props yoked together
- IF 3+ commas/ands → fundamental flaw
- IF any competitor could say it → not a trueline, it's a category descriptor

### 17-Step Zag Framework
**Source**: Zag (2006)

**Purpose**: Has the brand systematically designed differentiation from purpose through portfolio protection?

Steps 6, 11, 17 are covered by dedicated frameworks (Onlyness, Trueline, C-Sickness). Steps 12-15 are execution/process steps. Below are the remaining evaluable steps.

#### Components
- **Step 1: Purpose** — Core purpose in ≤12 words. Generic ('provide value to stakeholders') = fail. Specific/passionate = pass.
  - Key questions:
    - Can the purpose be stated in 12 words or less?
    - Is it specific and passionate or generic?
- **Step 2: Category** — Category name customers recognize. Too broad = meaningless. Made-up = unrecognizable.
  - Key questions:
    - Is the category recognizable to customers?
    - Is it too broad or too invented?
- **Step 3: Vision** — Concrete picturable future, not abstract.
  - Key questions:
    - Is the vision concrete and picturable?
    - 'Man on moon by 1960s' vs 'advancing capabilities'?
- **Step 4: Trend** — Named macro/cultural trend the brand authentically rides.
  - Key questions:
    - Is a specific trend named?
    - Does the brand authentically ride it?
    - If every competitor rides the same trend, it negates differentiation.
- **Step 5: Brandscape** — Competitive map with birth order, market share, honest rank.
  - Key questions:
    - Is there a competitive map?
    - Is the brand's rank honestly acknowledged?
    - 'No competitors' = automatic fail.
- **Step 7: Add/Subtract** — What to add to reinforce the zag AND (critically) what to subtract.
  - Key questions:
    - What's being added to reinforce the zag?
    - What's being subtracted? Only adding, never subtracting = weakness.
- **Step 8: Tribe** — Unique Buying Tribe defined by shared identity/aims, not demographics.
  - Key questions:
    - Is the tribe defined by identity markers?
    - 'If I buy this, I become ___.'
    - 'Everyone' = fail.
- **Step 9: Enemy** — Named competing brand or behavior as contrast. 'Not to topple big guys but to throw your zag into sharp relief.'
  - Key questions:
    - Is there a named enemy (brand or behavior)?
    - Is it specific and vivid?
    - Vague 'the competition' = weak.
- **Step 10: Naming** — Seven criteria: Distinctiveness, Brevity (≤4 syllables), Appropriateness, Easy spelling/pronunciation, Likability, Extendability, Protectability.
  - Key questions:
    - Does the name pass all seven criteria?
- **Step 16: Extensions** — Stickiness limits stretchiness. Downmarket = dilutes.
  - Key questions:
    - Does each extension reinforce core meaning?
    - Is the brand being stretched beyond its stickiness?

#### Decision rules
- Steps 1-5 feed Step 6 (Onlyness). Step 6 feeds 7-11, 16, 17.
- Step 11 (Trueline) is direct output of Step 6.
- IF purpose is generic → brand lacks soul.
- IF 'no competitors' claimed → automatic fail on brandscape.
- IF only adding, never subtracting → weakness in zag design.

### Brand Commitment Matrix (IAM/POV)
**Source**: The Brand Flip (2015)

**Purpose**: Is the brand's purpose/onlyness/values aligned with what the customer tribe needs?

Three-row alignment check between customer needs and brand offerings.

#### Components
- **Identity ↔ Purpose** — Customer asks 'who am I becoming?' Brand answers with purpose ('why do we exist?').
  - Key questions:
    - Does the brand's purpose empower the customer's desired identity?
- **Aims ↔ Onlyness** — Customer asks 'what do I want to achieve?' Brand answers with onlyness ('what do we uniquely offer?').
  - Key questions:
    - Does the brand's onlyness serve the customer's goals?
- **Mores ↔ Values** — Customer asks 'what community/behavior?' Brand answers with values ('how do we behave?').
  - Key questions:
    - Do brand values match tribe behavioral norms?

#### Decision rules
- Any misaligned row = structural positioning flaw.
- Purpose that doesn't empower identity → brand exists for itself.
- Onlyness that doesn't serve aims → unique but unwanted.
- Values that contradict mores → trust crisis.

### C-Sickness Diagnostic
**Source**: Zag (2006) Step 17

**Purpose**: Is the brand portfolio compromised by extension diseases?

Four portfolio diseases that erode brand positioning.

#### Components
- **Contagion** — Problem in one brand cascades to all. Dark side of synergy. Too tightly linked = cross-contamination risk.
  - Key questions:
    - Are brands too tightly linked such that a problem in one cascades to all?
- **Confusion** — Extended past customer-drawn boundaries. Customers can't state what brand means.
  - Key questions:
    - Can customers clearly state what the brand means?
    - 'Customers want choice AMONG brands, not WITHIN brands.'
- **Contradiction** — Means X in one context, Y in another. Fragmented identity across regions/segments/channels.
  - Key questions:
    - Does the brand mean different things in different contexts?
    - Is identity fragmented across regions/segments/channels?
- **Complexity** — Overgrown portfolio, too many brands/sub-brands, resources spread thin, roles unclear.
  - Key questions:
    - Is the portfolio overgrown?
    - Are brand roles clear?
    - Are resources spread thin?

#### Decision rules
- IF any disease present → portfolio-level flag
- IF Confusion → Onlyness has been compromised by extensions
- IF Contradiction undermines Onlyness statement → fundamental flaw
- IF Complexity → subtraction required before new positioning work

### Brand Validation Tests
**Source**: The Brand Gap (2003/2006)

**Purpose**: Are brand elements distinctive and coherent in execution?

Four tests for brand distinctiveness and coherence.

#### Components
- **Swap Test** — Swap trademark/icon with competitor's. If theirs looks better or no worse → identity needs work.
  - Key questions:
    - If you swap the trademark/icon with a competitor's, does it matter?
- **Hand Test** — Cover logo on any communication. If you can't tell whose it is → voice is not distinctive.
  - Key questions:
    - With the logo covered, can you tell whose communication this is?
- **Concept Test** — Show prototypes to 10+ real audience members. Ask comprehension questions (not preference).
  - Key questions:
    - Can audience members describe the brand accurately?
- **Field Test** — Place prototype in real environment among competitors. Measures distinctiveness, relevance, memorability, extendability, depth.
  - Key questions:
    - Does the brand stand out in its real competitive environment?

#### Decision rules
- Swap and Hand tests are applicable from documents.
- Concept and Field tests require audience access.
- Failing both Swap and Hand → brand expressions are interchangeable with competitors.

### Good-Different Chart
**Source**: Zag (2006)

**Purpose**: Initial diagnostic: which quadrant does the brand occupy?

Two-axis chart. Y = Good (quality/value). X = Different (novelty/distinctiveness).

#### Components
- **Upper Right: Good AND Different** — Successful zag. Target quadrant. (Aeron Chair, Prius, Cirque du Soleil)
  - Key questions:
    - Is the brand both good AND different?
- **Upper Left: Good but NOT Different** — Tests well, will be commoditized. Needs Neumeier urgently.
  - Key questions:
    - Is the brand good but not differentiated? Commoditization risk?
- **Lower Right: Different but NOT Good** — High risk, sometimes breakthrough territory.
  - Key questions:
    - Is the brand different but quality/value is lacking?
- **Lower Left: Neither** — Dead on arrival.
  - Key questions:
    - Is the brand neither good nor different?

#### Decision rules
- Target is always upper-right (Good AND Different).
- Upper-left (Good but not Different) is the most common problem Neumeier addresses.
- Lower-right is high-risk but sometimes breakthrough territory.

### Scissors/Paper/Rock
**Source**: Zag (2006)

**Purpose**: Identify competitive stage to calibrate which frameworks matter most.

Three competitive stages mapped to brand priorities.

#### Components
- **Scissors (Startup)** — Sharp focus, one brand. Cuts white space. Vulnerable to resource-rich crushers.
  - Key questions:
    - Is focus being protected?
    - Is the brand cutting into white space?
- **Rock (Mid-size)** — Momentum. More brands, less focus. Priority: maintain differentiation during growth.
  - Key questions:
    - Is differentiation being maintained during growth?
    - Is focus eroding with scale?
- **Paper (Large)** — Scale/network effects. Least focus. Priority: C-Sickness prevention. Most vulnerable to niche attackers.
  - Key questions:
    - Is C-Sickness being prevented?
    - Is the brand vulnerable to niche attackers?

#### Decision rules
- Scissors: protect focus above all.
- Rock: maintain differentiation during growth.
- Paper: prevent C-Sickness. Most vulnerable to niche attackers (scissors).
